# Custom Solutions Strategy

## Purpose

This document describes how ai-aarti.com can grow from an evidence-led portfolio into a trusted custom-solutions practice that creates real value before it asks anyone to pay. It is a product and operating blueprint, not legal, tax, safeguarding, financial, or professional-services advice.

The goal is not to turn every visitor into a customer. The goal is to make the right work easy to understand, easy to request, safely scoped, and reliably delivered.

## The strategic shift

### Today

The website establishes credibility through public projects, research notes, a collaboration pathway, and transparent limits on evidence.

### Future state

The website becomes a **custom solutions studio for schools, educators, community groups, student teams, and mission-led small organizations**. It helps those groups turn difficult-to-understand information, workflows, or learning challenges into clear, accessible digital tools and evidence-led communication.

### The value proposition

> Turn a real community, learning, or information challenge into a clear, usable, responsibly built solution—with visible evidence, documentation, and a plan for what happens after launch.

This positioning is stronger than offering “websites,” “apps,” or “AI services” in the abstract. It names a useful outcome and preserves the portfolio’s research-informed, human-centered identity.

## Who the practice should serve

Start with audiences where the existing portfolio provides authentic proof and where small, focused engagements can deliver an observable result.

| Primary audience | Typical problem | High-value result | Relevant existing proof |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Educators and student programs | A resource, project, or process is hard for students to use | A clear learning tool, project hub, or resource guide | Student productivity, math/science exploration, research writing |
| Community organizations | Important information is fragmented or difficult to explain | An accessible information hub, intake flow, or public explainer | Civic, transit, charitable, and public-information work |
| Research and science groups | Evidence exists but is not legible to a broad audience | A research story, dashboard, interactive explainer, or reproducible project page | Urban heat, spectral, framework, technical documentation work |
| Student teams and clubs | Knowledge and handoffs are trapped in people or chats | A sustainable team site, playbook, onboarding, or lightweight tool | Robotics, open-source, documentation architecture |
| Mission-led small organizations | Their website does not clarify trust, offers, or next steps | A conversion-ready, accessible web presence with a maintainable content system | This portfolio’s CMS, SEO, and collaboration system |

Avoid presenting the practice as a substitute for clinical care, legal advice, financial advice, emergency support, security consulting, or regulated professional services. Some portfolio themes touch wellbeing and health; those should remain educational, evidence-aware, and appropriately supervised.

## Productized offer ladder

Custom work becomes easier to sell and deliver when it begins with a few named engagement types. Each offer below has a clear finish line. A client can later move to a larger engagement only after receiving value from the first one.

| Offer | Buyer outcome | Typical deliverables | Best first customers | Payment model |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Clarity Sprint | A shared view of the problem and the smallest valuable next step | discovery workshop, audience map, problem statement, options, prioritized roadmap | educators, nonprofits, student teams | fixed fee; optionally sponsored or pro bono early on |
| Evidence-to-Experience Blueprint | A research or information topic becomes a build-ready experience | content model, information architecture, wireframes, accessibility notes, measurement plan | research projects, public information initiatives | fixed fee |
| Launch-Ready Information Hub | People can find, understand, and act on key information | responsive site or portal, CMS setup, SEO basics, analytics plan, editor guide | community organizations, programs, small teams | fixed scope with milestone payments |
| Learning Tool or Interactive Explainer | Learners can practice, explore, or understand a difficult concept | interactive prototype, teacher/facilitator notes, usability feedback summary, source notes | teachers, clubs, research outreach teams | fixed scope or sponsored project |
| Systems Stewardship | A useful solution stays current and understandable | monthly updates, content support, analytics review, backlog triage, quarterly improvement memo | organizations with an existing hub | recurring retainer after a completed launch |

### What not to sell first

- Unlimited custom development.
- Vague “AI transformation” projects.
- A full marketplace, course platform, or social network before demand is proven.
- High-stakes data systems that store student records, health data, payment details, or sensitive personal information.
- Outcome guarantees such as increased grades, donations, enrollment, revenue, wellbeing, or search ranking.

These create uncontrolled scope and risk before the delivery operation is mature.

## What makes the offers genuinely valuable

Every engagement should include more than a polished interface. The minimum value standard is:

1. **A clear audience and decision.** Identify who must understand or do something differently.
2. **Evidence and constraints.** Preserve sources, assumptions, dependencies, and what is not yet known.
3. **A usable artifact.** Deliver a site, workflow, prototype, guide, or system that can actually be used.
4. **Accessibility and clarity.** Make the work navigable on mobile, keyboard-friendly, plain-language where appropriate, and understandable without insider knowledge.
5. **Ownership and continuity.** Leave the customer with editable content, documentation, access ownership, and a next-step backlog.
6. **Measurement.** Define a small number of ethical signals that show whether the tool is helping.

The differentiator is therefore not merely design or code. It is the combination of **research discipline, clear communication, responsible scope, and maintainable implementation**.

## The custom-solutions delivery system

Use a consistent lifecycle. It protects the customer from ambiguity and protects the practice from unpaid, endless revision cycles.

```text
Discover → Qualify → Scope → Agree → Build → Review → Launch → Learn → Extend or close
```

### 1. Discover

The website should offer a short, low-pressure starting path. It asks for the audience, challenge, desired outcome, timeline, public links, budget range if appropriate, and whether an adult/institutional contact must be involved.

Deliverable: a concise discovery record, not free consulting. A first conversation should end with either a clear next action, a helpful redirect, or a respectful “not a fit.”

### 2. Qualify

Evaluate each request using a visible internal scorecard.

| Criterion | Questions | Go / no-go signal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mission fit | Does the work improve learning, community clarity, research communication, or a mission-led workflow? | The project has a meaningful public or user benefit. |
| Evidence readiness | Is there enough source material and a decision-maker available? | The client can supply facts and approve content. |
| Scope | Can a useful first version ship in a bounded timeframe? | A minimum viable outcome can be named. |
| Safety | Does it avoid sensitive data, regulated claims, and unsupervised high-stakes decisions? | Risks are understood and manageable. |
| Capacity | Can the work be completed without harming school, wellbeing, or existing commitments? | Time and responsible supervision exist. |
| Commercial viability | Is there a fair funding route, sponsor, grant, or customer budget? | The exchange is clear and documented. |

### 3. Scope

Write a one-page solution brief before quoting or building:

- Customer and project owner.
- User and user problem.
- Desired outcome and success signal.
- In-scope deliverables.
- Explicit exclusions.
- Inputs the customer must provide.
- Timeline and review points.
- Accessibility, privacy, and content requirements.
- Acceptance criteria.
- Ownership, hosting, and maintenance plan.

### 4. Agree

Do not start paid work from an email thread alone. Use an age-appropriate, guardian-reviewed agreement and invoice process before accepting money or handling intellectual property. The agreement should say who can approve work, who owns which assets, how revisions work, what happens when a dependency is late, and how either side can end the project.

### 5. Build

Work in visible weekly increments. A simple cadence is:

- Week start: state the intended result and dependencies.
- Midweek: share one inspectable artifact or decision.
- Review: collect consolidated feedback from the named approver.
- Week end: update the decision log, status, risks, and next milestone.

### 6. Review and launch

Use acceptance criteria, not “looks good.” For a public site, this normally includes responsive review, keyboard navigation, basic performance checks, link checks, approved copy, analytics consent configuration, ownership handoff, and a rollback plan.

### 7. Learn

After launch, report only useful, privacy-preserving signals. Examples: key resource clicks, completed inquiry starts, successful form submissions, time to find a resource, editor update completion, and qualitative user feedback. Do not overclaim causality from a small amount of data.

## Lead capture and qualification design

The current collaboration API is a solid foundation: it has validation, a honeypot, rate limiting, idempotent Resend delivery, timeouts, and a webhook fallback. Reuse its reliability patterns, but create a distinct solution-inquiry model.

### Proposed solution inquiry fields

Keep the first submission short. Fields marked “conditional” appear only after the visitor chooses a path.

| Field | Required | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Name and work email | Yes | Enables a reply and accountability. |
| Organization or project | Yes | Provides context. |
| Organization type | Yes | Enables relevant routing. |
| What needs to become easier or clearer? | Yes | Captures the actual problem, not a requested feature. |
| Who is it for? | Yes | Grounds the solution in users. |
| Desired outcome | Yes | Establishes a finish line. |
| Timeline or decision date | Conditional | Needed only when a real deadline exists. |
| Public links or materials | Optional | Lets the visitor provide context without forcing it. |
| Budget range | Optional at first; required before a proposal | Allows respectful qualification without blocking early conversations. |
| Adult/institutional project contact | Conditional | Required where a student-led engagement needs oversight. |
| Working-agreement acknowledgement | Yes | Sets safety and communication boundaries. |

Never request passwords, financial account information, student records, medical information, government IDs, confidential documents, or private access credentials through a public form.

### Internal states

Use a simple private tracker with one owner per inquiry:

```text
New → Needs context → Fit call proposed → Scoped → Proposal sent → Accepted → Active → Awaiting client → Launched → Follow-up → Closed / Referred
```

Each record should contain the request ID, contact, source, fit score, risk notes, next action, owner, and last-contact date. Avoid putting form text into analytics tools or session replay.

## Pricing and commercial model

### Principles

- Charge for a defined outcome, not elapsed chat time.
- Begin with fixed-fee, bounded work.
- Use a paid discovery or clarity sprint when the problem is not yet ready to quote.
- Tie payments to milestones and accepted deliverables.
- Include a limited revision cycle in every proposal; quote scope changes separately.
- Price based on the value, risk, and responsibility of the work—not on the customer’s assumptions about a student’s age.
- Never underprice work that needs professional oversight just to win a project.

### A safe pricing progression

| Stage | Commercial approach | Evidence required before advancing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Validation | pro bono, school-sponsored, or grant-funded pilots with strict scope | two to three completed projects and feedback |
| Early paid work | fixed-fee discovery and small implementations | repeatable delivery, written case studies, guardian/legal process |
| Established practice | outcome-based packages plus maintenance retainers | clear demand, referral flow, capacity plan, reliable margins |
| Scaled offering | templates, workshops, licensed resources, or a focused software product | recurring common need proven across multiple customers |

Publish “starting from” prices only after the delivery time, supervision costs, taxes, and revision patterns are known. Before that, use honest language: “Most work begins with a scoped clarity sprint. A written estimate follows after fit and requirements are clear.”

### Revenue mix to aim for later

Avoid depending on a single large custom project. A resilient mix can include:

- 50–60% fixed-scope custom solutions.
- 20–30% ongoing stewardship and improvement retainers.
- 10–20% workshops, audits, or training.
- 0–20% reusable templates, guides, or a focused software product—only after validation.

These are strategic ranges, not a forecast.

## Case studies: the sales asset that preserves integrity

Portfolio repositories are useful proof, but buyers need a story about the outcome. Build a standard case-study template and use it only with accurate, approved information.

### Required sections

1. **Situation:** Who had what problem?
2. **Constraint:** What made it difficult?
3. **Approach:** What research, design, and implementation choices were made?
4. **Artifact:** What was delivered? Link to public work where appropriate.
5. **Result:** What was observed? Separate measured results from qualitative feedback.
6. **Limitations:** What does the result not prove?
7. **Next step:** What should happen after the pilot or launch?
8. **Permission:** What details, logo, quote, and links have been approved for publication?

### Evidence standard

| Claim type | Acceptable evidence |
| --- | --- |
| “Built” | public URL, repository, screenshot, release note, or client confirmation |
| “Used by” | customer confirmation and a clearly defined audience/time period |
| “Improved” | before/after measure, defined method, and caveats |
| “Saved time” | workflow timing comparison with sample size and assumptions |
| “Loved by users” | attributed, authorized quote; do not infer universal satisfaction |

Use “pilot,” “prototype,” “exploration,” and “early feedback” when those are the honest descriptions.

## Technical implementation roadmap

### Phase 1 — Foundation: make custom work understandable

**Objective:** Add a useful, trustworthy solutions pathway without accepting payment.

- Create the `/solutions` route and Puck content model.
- Create a reusable `OfferGrid` block with audience, outcome, deliverables, fit, and CTA fields.
- Create a reusable `CaseStudy` block with an evidence/limitation field that cannot be omitted.
- Create `/work-with-aarti` with a distinct qualification form and a working-agreement acknowledgement.
- Add a server route such as `/api/solution-inquiries`; keep form data out of analytics.
- Add source tags to the inquiry route: `solutions_home`, `case_study`, `offer_page`, `direct`.
- Add a server-side notification destination and private triage process.
- Add a public working agreement, privacy update, and accessibility statement after appropriate review.

**Exit criteria:** A qualified visitor can understand what is offered, determine fit, submit a safe inquiry, and receive an honest acknowledgement.

### Phase 2 — Proof: turn work into repeatable trust

**Objective:** Build a small library of verified proof.

- Complete 2–3 tightly scoped pilot engagements.
- Capture an approved case study for each.
- Add a post-project feedback form that asks about process, usefulness, clarity, and permission to quote.
- Add a lightweight results dashboard that shows only aggregate, consented, non-sensitive signals.
- Refine offers based on which problems repeat and which projects produce the strongest outcomes.

**Exit criteria:** Each named offer has at least one credible example, a realistic timeline, and a documented handoff process.

### Phase 3 — Commercial readiness: accept paid engagements responsibly

**Objective:** Establish the operational system before adding checkout.

- Obtain guardian/legal/accounting guidance appropriate to the business structure and location.
- Decide who can sign agreements, receive payment, own accounts, and approve client communication.
- Set up invoicing through a reputable provider under the approved business arrangement.
- Establish proposal, statement-of-work, change-order, and invoice templates.
- Define a secure customer communication channel and access-transfer checklist.
- Add a proposal acceptance flow; do not collect payment on the public inquiry form.

**Exit criteria:** A paid project can be agreed, invoiced, delivered, handed off, and closed without improvised legal, financial, or access-control decisions.

### Phase 4 — Recurring value: stewardship and focused products

**Objective:** Create compounding value beyond one-off projects.

- Offer monthly content/system stewardship only to completed project customers.
- Identify the single most repeated customer problem.
- Test a workshop, template, toolkit, or paid audit before building a SaaS product.
- Use customer interviews and paid pilots to validate willingness to pay.
- Build a product only when repeated workflows, user roles, data boundaries, and support needs are clear.

**Exit criteria:** Recurring revenue comes from an established need and does not depend on adding features faster than they can be supported.

The site already supports Microsoft Clarity and PostHog, but the README correctly notes privacy constraints. Before enabling either, confirm that the audience, consent posture, privacy policy, and data settings are appropriate—particularly because the site represents a student.

### Events worth measuring

| Event | Purpose | Never include |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `solutions_page_viewed` | Interest by content route | name, email, query string with personal data |
| `offer_selected` | Which outcome resonates | form text or identifiers |
| `case_study_opened` | Proof content engagement | visitor identity |
| `solution_inquiry_started` | Funnel health | typed values |
| `solution_inquiry_submitted` | Qualified conversion count | submitted fields |
| `resource_downloaded` | Resource usefulness | email unless explicit consent and separate system |
| `proposal_accepted` | Operational conversion; record server-side | payment or agreement details in client analytics |

### Core metrics

- Qualified inquiry rate: qualified inquiries / solution-page visitors.
- Discovery-to-proposal rate.
- Proposal acceptance rate.
- Time from inquiry to first useful response.
- Time from agreement to launch.
- Scope-change rate.
- Post-project usefulness score and permissioned testimonial rate.
- Monthly recurring revenue share, only after paid operations begin.

Use these metrics to improve service design, not to pressure visitors or infer sensitive information.

## Safeguarding, privacy, and legal-operational gates

Because this is a student-led public business and portfolio, these gates are prerequisites—not later polish.

1. **Responsible adult and institutional coordination:** Establish who reviews commercial commitments, meetings, payments, and client access.
2. **Contracting authority:** Obtain local legal guidance on who may sign client agreements and receive revenue.
3. **Data minimization:** Design services to avoid collecting or processing personal, student, health, financial, or confidential data unless there is an explicitly reviewed lawful basis and appropriate controls.
4. **Meeting safety:** Use approved communication channels; establish meeting boundaries and attendance expectations.
5. **Intellectual property:** Clarify ownership of client content, custom code, reusable components, open-source dependencies, and portfolio publication rights.
6. **Accessibility:** Use a documented standard and test each public deliverable before launch.
7. **Insurance and liability:** Seek qualified guidance before taking on work with meaningful operational or reputational risk.
8. **Financial operations:** Use a legitimate business payment and accounting process; do not collect card or banking details directly through the site.

Do not publish legal promises or accept paid work until the appropriate adults and qualified advisors have reviewed the operational model.

## First 90 days: a practical action plan

### Days 1–30: define and validate

- Interview 8–12 potential users across educators, community organizations, and student teams.
- Ask about their current problem, cost of inaction, existing workaround, decision-maker, budget process, and what a useful result would look like.
- Choose one primary offer, ideally the Clarity Sprint or Evidence-to-Experience Blueprint.
- Draft the solution brief, discovery agenda, working agreement, and case-study template.
- Build `/solutions` and `/work-with-aarti` without payment collection.

### Days 31–60: deliver a bounded pilot

- Select one mission-aligned, safe, scoped pilot with an engaged approver.
- Run discovery, produce a written brief, and agree on a definition of done.
- Deliver a small but real artifact.
- Conduct a usability or stakeholder review.
- Capture outcomes, limitations, and permission for a case study.

### Days 61–90: package what worked

- Publish one evidence-led case study.
- Tighten the offer page around the observed customer problem and result.
- Add a simple inquiry qualification flow and private tracker.
- Decide what must be in place before accepting a paid engagement.
- Set the next pilot or first paid discovery target only after capacity and safeguards are confirmed.

## Decision rules

Use these rules to stay focused as opportunities grow.

- Say yes when the problem is clear, the user benefit is meaningful, the scope is bounded, and the work can be responsibly supported.
- Start with discovery when a buyer asks for features but cannot state the user problem or decision to improve.
- Refer or decline when the work requires regulated expertise, sensitive-data handling, unsafe access, unrealistic deadlines, or commitments beyond capacity.
- Productize only after the same customer problem appears at least three times with similar users, workflow, and willingness to pay.
- Add payment only after agreements, supervision, invoicing, tax/accounting, privacy, and handoff processes are ready.

## Immediate next recommendation

Build the **Solutions** pathway and run one rigorously documented, low-risk pilot before adding a pricing page or checkout. The first commercial asset should be a credible case study and a repeatable delivery process—not a payment button.
